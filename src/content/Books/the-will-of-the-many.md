### Overview
The Will of the Many is the opening volume of The Hierarchy trilogy by Australian bestselling author James Islington, whose previous Licanius Trilogy (2014–2019) established him as a major voice in modern epic fantasy. Set in a world heavily inspired by the Roman Republic, the novel follows a deposed prince hiding in plain sight inside the empire that destroyed his family, navigating a prestigious academy where the next generation of leaders is forged — and where secrets lurk that could tear the republic apart. The book was met with immediate critical acclaim, earning a starred review from Library Journal, which called it a "brilliant and gut-churning masterpiece," and Publishers Weekly, which praised its "exceptionally detailed and thoughtful" worldbuilding and "perfectly balanced" character development and plot momentum.
The novel's Latin-flavoured tagline captures its ethos precisely: AUDI. VIDE. TACE. — Listen. See. Be silent.
---
### Setting: The Catenan Republic
The story unfolds within the Catenan Republic, known to its subjects simply as the Hierarchy — a vast, expansionist empire that has spent centuries conquering neighbouring kingdoms and absorbing them into a rigidly stratified social order. The Republic is modelled explicitly on the Roman Republic, complete with a Senate, consular titles, Latinised nomenclature, and a culture of civic duty that masks deep systemic cruelty.
The Republic's defining institution is the Will system. Every citizen is expected to eventually perform a ritual at an Aurora Columnae, voluntarily ceding a portion of their personal energy — their drive, their focus, their strength — which they call Will — to those above them in the social hierarchy. This siphoned Will flows upward through the ranks, concentrating power in the hands of senators and the ruling elite. Millions comply. Refusal means exclusion from civilised society, harassment, and worse. The system is presented as a fair exchange — the lower classes give Will, and in return receive the benefits of Roman-style infrastructure, order, and protection — but the novel makes clear that this is coercion dressed in the language of consent.
The Republic's most recent conquest is Suus, a small island kingdom whose ruling family was executed upon surrender. This is the conquest that shapes everything that follows.
Principal Characters
---
### Vis Telimus / Prince Diago of Suus
The protagonist and first-person narrator. Vis's real name is Diago, the last surviving prince of Suus. He was seventeen when the Republic invaded and killed his entire family; he survived by disappearing into the system he hates most — living as an anonymous orphan in a brutal state-run orphanage, working in a prison where Sappers drain inmates' Will for redistribution to the wealthy. By night, he fights in illegal underground bouts to earn money and vent his rage. He refuses to perform the Will ritual, enduring beatings and ostracism as a result. When he is adopted by Senator Ulciscor Telimus and enrolled in the Catenan Academy, he must pretend to be an ordinary orphan from Aquiria — another conquered territory — while secretly pursuing multiple agendas: solving a murder, searching for an ancient weapon, and concealing his royal identity from an empire that would execute him on sight.
---
### Senator Ulciscor Telimus
The man who adopts Vis. Ulciscor is a powerful Military senator with his own hidden agenda: his brother Caeror died at the Academy under mysterious circumstances, and Ulciscor wants Vis to find out why. Ulciscor is calculating, politically shrewd, and genuinely dangerous — but he is also Vis's only shield against discovery. Their relationship is transactional on the surface and far more complex beneath it.
---
### Callidus
Vis's closest friend at the Academy. A warm, loyal, and perceptive young man whose friendship becomes one of Vis's deepest anchors — and whose fate becomes one of the novel's most emotionally devastating threads.
---
### Aequa
A fellow student who befriends Vis early and accompanies him to a pivotal local festival. She is perceptive, brave, and one of the few people at the Academy whose motives appear genuinely uncomplicated — which, in this world, makes her both precious and imperilled.
---
### Eidhin
A sharp-witted classmate who becomes a key ally. Eidhin offers Vis pragmatic counsel about when to compromise and when to hold firm, including the observation that "pride and self-respect may mean we never give in, but if they are all our enemies, we will never be happy."
---
### Lanistia
A woman who trains Vis for the Academy's physical and tactical challenges. She was close friends with the murdered Caeror and lost her eyes in the same incident that killed him. She is a living reminder of the cost of the secrets Vis is trying to uncover.
Estevan
The leader of the Anguis, a resistance organisation dedicated to dismantling the Republic from within. A former citizen of Suus, Estevan is willing to use catastrophic violence — including a mysterious weapon that slaughters thousands at a public festival — to achieve his goals. He forces Vis into an impossible choice: join the resistance, or be exposed. He then dies by suicide in a way that frames Vis as his killer, making Vis simultaneously a hero to the Republic and a target for the Anguis.

### Plot Summary
Part One: The Orphan and the Senator
Vis works in a Catenan prison, monitoring Sappers — devices that drain Will from inmates. He endures regular beatings at the orphanage for his refusal to cede his own Will. When a stranger arrives at the prison to question an inmate in a dead language, Vis is intrigued. The stranger then appears at the orphanage and reveals himself as Senator Ulciscor Telimus, adopting Vis on the spot. The deal: Vis enters the Catenan Academy — the elite school where the Republic's future leaders are trained — and investigates the death of Ulciscor's brother Caeror. In exchange, Vis receives the protection of the Telimus name. Vis accepts, seeing an opportunity for both survival and the revenge he has been nurturing for three years.
Part Two: The Academy
The Catenan Academy is a crucible of competition, political manoeuvring, and physical danger. Students are ranked and sorted into disciplines — Governance, Military, Research, and others — based on performance in examinations, combat trials, and the opaque politics of patronage. Vis must rise through the ranks while concealing his identity, his motives, and his hatred of the very system he is being trained to serve.
He bonds with Callidus, Aequa, and Eidhin, building friendships that are genuine but freighted with deception — every connection Vis makes is shadowed by the lies he must tell to survive. He trains under Lanistia, who pushes him physically while carrying her own grief and suspicion about Caeror's death.
Meanwhile, the Anguis — the resistance group — makes contact. They know Vis's real identity as Diago of Suus, and they blackmail him: cooperate with them, or be exposed. Vis is now a triple agent — serving Ulciscor's agenda, the Anguis's agenda, and his own.
Part Three: The Festival and Its Aftermath
Vis attends a local festival with Aequa. There, Estevan, leader of the Anguis, activates a mysterious weapon that kills thousands of people in an instant. Vis, horrified by the indiscriminate slaughter despite his hatred of the Republic, confronts Estevan. Estevan tells Vis that he must choose a side — there is no neutral ground — and then dies by suicide, arranging the scene so that it appears Vis killed him.
The consequences are immediate and contradictory: the Republic hails Vis as a hero who stopped a terrorist; the Anguis marks him as a traitor who murdered their leader. Vis's position, already precarious, becomes a razor's edge. He must continue climbing the Academy's ranks, maintaining the trust of his friends and the Republic, while the Anguis hunts him and the secrets he was sent to uncover grow darker and more dangerous with every layer he peels back.
The novel culminates in the Iudicium, a brutal final trial in which Vis runs the Labyrinth — a deadly physical and psychological challenge that determines final rankings. Vis loses an arm in the process but survives, and in doing so acquires new abilities tied to the Will that mark him as Synchronous — a person with versions of himself existing across three parallel worlds. This revelation opens the door to the sequel and reframes the novel's worldbuilding in startling ways.

### Themes
Resistance and Complicity: The novel's central ethical tension. Vis hates the Republic and everything it stands for, yet he must participate in its systems — attend its school, befriend its children, advance its ranks — to survive and to pursue his goals. When does working within an unjust system become indistinguishable from supporting it? Is there a meaningful difference between strategic compliance and genuine complicity?
Greed and the Corruptibility of Governance: The Will system is a literalisation of economic exploitation — the powerful feed on the energy of the powerless, and the powerless are told it is for their own good. The Republic's rhetoric of civilisation and mutual benefit is systematically exposed as a mechanism for extraction and control.
The Power of Friendship and Loyalty: Vis's greatest strength is not his intelligence or his combat skill but his ability to inspire genuine loyalty. Callidus, Aequa, and Eidhin become his moral anchors — and his greatest vulnerability, because every secret he keeps from them is a betrayal of the trust they offer freely.
Identity and the Masks We Wear: Vis is Diago, Vis, Catenicus — a prince, an orphan, a senator's ward, a resistance pawn. The novel interrogates whether any of these identities is more "real" than the others, and what it costs a person to live inside a lie for years.
Dead Languages and Cultural Erasure: The Republic systematically erases the languages and cultures of conquered peoples. Vis's knowledge of dead languages like Cymrian and Vetusian becomes both a tool and a symbol — a connection to the world the Republic destroyed and a weapon the Republic cannot anticipate.

### About the Author
James Islington was born and raised in southern Victoria, Australia. He grew up reading Raymond E. Feist and Robert Jordan, and later, discovering Brandon Sanderson and Patrick Rothfuss, was inspired to begin writing himself. His debut, The Shadow of What Was Lost (2014), launched the Licanius Trilogy, which was completed with An Echo of Things to Come (2017) and The Light of All That Falls (2019). The trilogy was praised for its intricate plotting, time-travel mechanics, and satisfying three-volume arc. The Will of the Many (2023) marked the beginning of his second major series, The Hierarchy, whose second volume, The Strength of the Few, was published in 2025. Islington lives with his wife and children in Victoria, Australia.

### Reception
The Will of the Many was one of the most acclaimed fantasy novels of 2023. Publishers Weekly gave it a starred review, calling it "staggering" and praising its "exceptionally detailed and thoughtful" worldbuilding. Library Journal awarded it another starred review, calling it a "brilliant and gut-churning masterpiece." Readers and critics drew immediate comparisons to The Name of the Wind (academy setting, hidden identity), Red Rising (undercover protagonist infiltrating an oppressive hierarchy), and The Roman Republic (political structure and Latinised culture). The novel was particularly praised for sustaining near-700-page suspense without a wasted chapter, and for its magic system — the Will — which functions simultaneously as a fantasy mechanic and a political metaphor. Some readers noted structural similarities to established fantasy tropes (the orphan prince, the magic school, the chosen one), but the consensus was that Islington executes these elements with a precision and moral complexity that transcends their familiarity. The sequel, The Strength of the Few (2025), continued the story to further acclaim.
