### Overview
Assassin's Apprentice is the debut novel of Robin Hobb (the pseudonym of Margaret Astrid Lindholm Ogden) and the first book in The Farseer Trilogy, which itself opens the larger 16-book Realm of the Elderlings sequence. The novel is a character-driven, first-person retrospective narrative that follows the early life of FitzChivalry Farseer — a royal bastard raised in the halls of Buckkeep Castle — as he is secretly trained in the arts of assassination and learns to navigate the lethal politics of the Six Duchies.
---
### Setting
The story is set in and around the Six Duchies, a coastal kingdom ruled by the Farseer dynasty from their seat at Buckkeep Castle. The realm is under growing threat from Red-Ship Raiders, mysterious seaborne attackers who ravage coastal towns and perform a horrifying ritual called "Forging" — stripping victims of their humanity and leaving them as soulless, violent husks. The political landscape is further complicated by tensions among the royal family, neighbouring kingdoms, and the Mountain Kingdom to the inland.
---
### Plot Summary
A Boy Without a Name
The novel opens with the earliest memory of its narrator: a six-year-old boy with no name, brought by his maternal grandfather to the Farseer military outpost at Moonseye on the border of the Mountain Kingdom. The old man declares the child to be the illegitimate son of Prince Chivalry, the King-in-Waiting (heir to the throne). The boy is handed over to Prince Verity, Chivalry's younger brother, who places him in the care of Burrich, Chivalry's loyal stable master and right-hand man. Burrich gives the boy the name "Fitz" — a traditional label for a bastard.
The Fall of Prince Chivalry
Before Fitz even arrives at Buckkeep, news of his existence causes a scandal. Prince Chivalry, unwilling to let uncertainty about his bastard son threaten the succession, abdicates his position as King-in-Waiting and retires to the royal estate at Withywoods with his wife, Lady Patience. Chivalry never meets Fitz. The boy is thus left fatherless in a castle where he is despised by many — most notably by Prince Regal, the youngest and most ambitious of the royal brothers, who sees Fitz as a threat and a humiliation to the family name.
---
### Growing Up in the Stables
Burrich raises Fitz as a stable boy, teaching him horsemanship, animal care, and a rough code of discipline. Fitz finds solace in the company of animals — particularly a puppy named Nosy, with whom he shares an instinctive telepathic bond. This bond is a manifestation of the Wit, an ancient and widely feared magic that allows its user to communicate mind-to-mind with animals. When Burrich discovers this connection, he reacts with horror and disgust, taking Nosy away and warning Fitz never to use the Wit again, as it is considered a perversion across the Six Duchies. This loss leaves Fitz deeply scarred and isolated.
The King's Secret Apprentice
Fitz's existence eventually draws the attention of King Shrewd, the ageing and shrewd ruler of the Six Duchies. Recognising both the danger and the potential usefulness of a royal bastard, the King arranges for Fitz to receive training in weaponry, scribing, and courtly manners. More secretly, he places Fitz under the tutelage of Chade Fallstar, a reclusive old assassin who has served the Farseer throne for decades in the shadows.
Chade teaches Fitz the craft of silent killing, poison, disguise, and observation. When Chade tests Fitz's loyalty by asking him to steal from the King — a ruse ordered by Shrewd himself — Fitz refuses, proving his steadfastness. The King then reveals the test and accepts Fitz as a weapon of the crown.
---
### The Skill and the Fool
Fitz is also introduced to the Skill, the hereditary magic of the Farseer bloodline — a telepathic and partially prophetic power that allows its users to communicate across distances and influence the minds of others. Fitz is sent to train under Galen, a harsh and politically aligned Skill master who despises him. Galen is secretly allied with Prince Regal's faction and actively tries to sabotage, discredit, and even kill Fitz during training. Despite this hostility, Fitz manages to develop a rudimentary grasp of the Skill.
During his time at Buckkeep, Fitz also encounters the Fool, the court jester — a pale, androgynous figure of mysterious origin who speaks in riddles and prophecies. The Fool becomes one of Fitz's few genuine friends and will go on to play a pivotal role throughout the entire Realm of the Elderlings series.
---
### The Red-Ship Crisis
As Fitz matures, the Red-Ship Raiders escalate their attacks on the coastal Duchies. Forged victims — people stripped of their souls — begin appearing in increasing numbers, sowing terror and social breakdown. King Shrewd, weakening with age and illness, struggles to hold the kingdom together. Prince Verity dedicates himself to defending the realm through the Skill, while Prince Regal schemes to consolidate power for himself, siphoning resources inland to his own holdings.
The Forging of Nosy and the Mountain Kingdom
In one of the novel's most emotionally devastating moments, Fitz encounters a group of Forged ones — soulless husks of former people — and discovers that among them is Nosy, the dog he bonded with as a child. Nosy, having been separated from Fitz and left to roam, was captured and Forged by the Raiders. This discovery forces Fitz to confront the full horror of the Red-Ship threat on a deeply personal level, and he is forced to put the creature down, ending the bond that was never truly severed.
---
### Fitz's First Mission
The King assigns Fitz his first true assassination mission: to eliminate Prince Rurisk of the Mountain Kingdom, who is allegedly plotting against the Six Duchies. Fitz travels to the Mountain Kingdom's capital, Jhaampe, where a diplomatic wedding between Prince Verity and Princess Kettricken of the Mountain Kingdom is to take place. There, Fitz discovers that Rurisk is not a traitor — the intelligence was manipulated, likely by agents of Prince Regal. Fitz must navigate a web of deception, choosing between his orders and his conscience. He ultimately refuses to kill Rurisk, who is instead murdered by another hand. Fitz survives a poisoning attempt and returns to Buckkeep, having completed his mission in body if not in spirit.
---
### The End of the Beginning
The novel closes with Fitz having survived his first mission but profoundly changed — no longer a child, yet far from a man in full control of his fate. Prince Chivalry is confirmed dead, murdered under mysterious circumstances. The kingdom teeters on the edge of collapse, with Regal's ambition growing, Verity stretched thin, and the Red-Ship raids showing no sign of abating. Fitz stands at the threshold of a life that will demand everything of him — his loyalty, his magic, his body, and his soul.
---
### Themes
Identity and Belonging: Fitz is caught between worlds — too royal to be common, too bastard-born to be acknowledged. His struggle for self-definition drives the entire narrative.
The Two Magics: The Skill (royal, sanctioned, political) and the Wit (ancient, feared, personal) represent the tension between public duty and private nature. The Wit's social stigma has been read as an allegory for marginalised identities.
Loyalty and Its Cost: Nearly every character demands Fitz's loyalty — the King, Burrich, Chade, Verity — yet few offer him full honesty or protection in return. The novel interrogates the morality of unquestioning service.
Coming of Age in Shadows: Unlike traditional fantasy heroes who quest for thrones and glory, Fitz is barred from the succession and trained to kill from the dark. His heroism is quiet, painful, and largely unrecognised.

### About the Author
Robin Hobb is the pseudonym of Margaret Astrid Lindholm Ogden, born in California in 1952. She previously published under the name Megan Lindholm before launching the Farseer Trilogy in 1995. Assassin's Apprentice was her first novel under the Hobb name and became a landmark in modern character-driven fantasy. She received the World Fantasy Award for Life Achievement in 2021. Her Realm of the Elderlings sequence spans five sub-series and 16 novels, published from 1995 to 2017.
---
### Reception
Assassin's Apprentice is widely regarded as a modern fantasy classic. George R. R. Martin praised Hobb's work, calling it "fantasy as it ought to be written." The Los Angeles Review of Books highlighted the "complete immersion in Fitz's complicated personality," and novelist Steven Erikson described the first-person narrative as a "quiet seduction." The novel is frequently cited for its psychological depth, nuanced moral ambiguity, and departure from the action-centric conventions of epic fantasy.
